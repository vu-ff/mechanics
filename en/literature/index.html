Lecture 1 · States, determinism and phase portraits

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §1 (determinacy) and §4 (one degree of freedom, pp. 15–22). Hirsch, Smale and Devaney, Differential Equations, Dynamical Systems, and an Introduction to Chaos, Chs. 7 and 9, for the example of §1.2 and the damped pendulum. Lecture 2 · Constraints and Lagrange’s equations

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §§17–18 (constraints and examples, pp. 75–83) and §21 (d’Alembert, pp. 91–97). Tong, Lectures on Classical Dynamics, §2.3 (constraints, pp. 17–21).

Lecture 3 · Hamilton’s principle and Noether’s theorem

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §§12–13 (pp. 55–61: variations and the Euler–Lagrange equations) and §20 (pp. 88–90: Noether’s theorem, whose figure Figure 3.3 follows). Tong, Lectures on Classical Dynamics, §2.1 (pp. 10–12: “a minimum or a saddle point”), §2.4 (pp. 23–26: Noether) and §2.5.7 (pp. 36–37: the charged particle and gauge freedom).

Lecture 4 · The Legendre transform and Hamilton’s equations

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §§14–15 (pp. 61–65). Cannas da Silva, Lectures on Symplectic Geometry, Lecture 20 (pp. 121–124). Deriglazov, Classical Mechanics, §2.1 (pp. 91–101; the phase paths after his Fig. 2.3). Tong, Lectures on Classical Dynamics, §4.1 (pp. 82–88). Fortney, A Visual Introduction to Differential Forms, Figs. 1.5–1.8 (pp. 14–16), for the picture of a covector.

Lecture 5 · Observables and the Poisson algebra

Takhtajan, Quantum Mechanics for Mathematicians, Ch. 1, §2.4 (pp. 38–39) and §2.6 (pp. 46–47). Tong, Lectures on Classical Dynamics, §4.3 (pp. 94–96).
