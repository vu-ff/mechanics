Lecture 11

Poisson manifolds and the rigid body

What this lecture does

Lecture 10 divided symmetries out of canonical systems. Dividing the rotations out of the rigid body completely leaves three variables, the components of its angular momentum, with a bracket whose matrix Π(x)\Pois(x)Π(x) varies from point to point and is singular everywhere. What survives when the bracket is such a matrix? A union of phase spaces: for the angular-momentum bracket, the spheres centred at the origin, and the origin. The body angular momentum M\MvecM of the free rigid body moves on those spheres, and on each of them its rotations about the axes of least and greatest inertia are stable and the rotation about the middle axis is not.

11.4 The rigid body and its sign

A rigid body with a point fixed at the origin has configuration space SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) (§2.2), so its phase space T∗SO(3)T^{*}\SO(3)T∗SO(3) is six-dimensional. Its kinetic energy is unchanged if the whole body is rotated about the fixed point, and for a free body the three components of its spatial angular momentum are conserved (§11.5 sketches why). Keeping only the angular momentum leaves three variables and a degenerate bracket on R3\R^{3}R3, as q×pq\times pq×p did. We state the result and do not prove it: nothing in this course builds T∗SO(3)T^{*}\SO(3)T∗SO(3).

The hypothesis is the only physical input, and we sketch why it holds for a free body. Let no external force act except the one that holds the fixed point, which has no torque about that point. The internal forces of a rigid body cancel in pairs along the lines joining the points — Newton’s third law in its strong form — so the total torque about the fixed point is zero and L\LvecL is constant.
