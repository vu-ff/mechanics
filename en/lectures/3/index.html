Lecture 3

Hamilton’s principle and Noether’s theorem

What this lecture does

Lecture 2 derived Lagrange’s equations from Newton’s law with ideal constraints. This lecture asks what single principle gives them, and why a symmetry gives a conserved quantity. The answer is that they are the conditions for one number, the action of a path, to be stationary. That is the course’s first answer to its question: a system is a configuration space with a Lagrangian, and a motion is a curve that makes the action stationary. The principle explains why the equations keep their form in every coordinate system, why the Lagrangian is not unique, what momentum is — the boundary term of the variation — and why every symmetry gives a conserved quantity (Noether). It closes with the energy function and the central force, whose motion is planar because angular momentum is conserved.

3.3 What the variational form buys

Covariance

Newton’s law mx¨=Fm\ddot x=Fmx¨=F changes its form in curvilinear coordinates: in polar coordinates a centrifugal term appears (Example 2.12). The Euler–Lagrange equations do not change their form, and the reason is that the action does not refer to coordinates.

Summary

  • The first answer: a system is a configuration space with a Lagrangian, and its motions are the paths that make the action stationary with fixed ends (Theorem 3.4) — stationary, not least (Proposition 3.5).
  • Handed on: the equations keep their form under point transformations (Theorem 3.6); adding dF/dt\dd F/\dd tdF/dt to L\LagL leaves them unchanged (Proposition 3.7); the momentum p=∂L/∂q˙p=\partial\Lag/\partial\dot qp=∂L/∂q˙​ is the boundary term of the variation (Proposition 3.10); a quasi-symmetry conserves p⋅ξ−Gp\cdot\xi-Gp⋅ξ−G (Theorem 3.13); the energy function ELE_{\Lag}EL​ (Theorem 3.14); the central force is planar (Proposition 3.15).
