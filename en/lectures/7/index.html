Lecture 7

States, Liouville’s theorem and the second answer

What this lecture does

Lectures 5 and 6 moved the observables along Hamiltonian flows. This lecture moves the states. A state need not be a point: incomplete knowledge is a probability distribution on phase space. To move one we need what the flow does to volume, and the answer — Hamiltonian flows preserve it — is Liouville’s theorem. States then move by the Liouville equation, and moving the states or moving the observables gives the same predictions. Volume also settles the question Lecture 1 asked about attractors. The lecture closes with the second answer to the course’s question: a classical mechanical system is a Poisson algebra of observables, a distinguished element HHH, and a convex set of states.

In words: with respect to the pairing ⟨ρ,f⟩=∫ρf\langle\rho,f\rangle=\int\rho f⟨ρ,f⟩=∫ρf, the generator {⋅,H}\pb\cdot H{⋅,H} of the Heisenberg equation and the generator {H,⋅}\pb H\cdot{H,⋅} of the Liouville equation are adjoint to one another. The Liouville equation is the adjoint of the Heisenberg equation — the classical ancestor of the relation between the Heisenberg and von Neumann equations.

7.4 No attractors

Lecture 1 asked what forbids attractors in mechanics. Volume settles the question, for attractors of every kind.

Definition 7.8 (Trapping region, attractor). A trapping region for a flow is a non-empty bounded open U⊆MU\subseteq\phaseU⊆M with U‾⊆M\overline U\subseteq\phaseU⊆M on which the flow is defined for all t≥0t\ge0t≥0 and satisfies φt(U)⊆U\flow t(U)\subseteq Uφt​(U)⊆U. The set it traps is Λ=⋂t≥0φt(U)\Lambda=\bigcap_{t\ge0}\flow t(U)Λ=⋂t≥0​φt​(U), and an attractor is a set of volume zero trapped by some trapping region. Sinks, attracting limit cycles and strange attractors are attractors in this sense.

Theorem 7.9 (Nothing is attracted proved modulo T2, T5 (cited)). Let UUU be a trapping region for XH\hvf HXH​. Then vol⁡(Λ)=vol⁡(U)>0\vol(\Lambda)=\vol(U)>0vol(Λ)=vol(U)>0. In particular a Hamiltonian flow has no attractor.

“Trapping” is not a technicality. The flow of H=−qpH=-qpH=−qp is φt(q,p)=(e−tq,etp)\flow t(q,p)=(e^{-t}q,e^{t}p)φt​(q,p)=(e−tq,etp), which preserves area while every point approaches the null line {q=0}\{q=0\}{q=0}. There is no trapping region: the orbits run off along ppp as they close in. Volume stops a flow compressing a region onto a thin set, not stretching one out along one.

This closes the question of §1.6. The damped pendulum has an attractor because its flow contracts volume at the rate γ\gammaγ; a Hamiltonian flow contracts nothing, so Theorem 7.9 rules out the sink, the attracting limit cycle and the strange attractor together. What a Hamiltonian flow can do instead is Figure 7.3: spread a blob out without shrinking it.

7.5 What a mechanical system is: the second answer

The question of Lecture 1 now has its second answer.

Definition 7.10 (Classical mechanical system, second answer). A classical mechanical system consists of

  1. a Poisson algebra A\mathcal AA of observables (Definition 5.7);

  2. a distinguished element H∈AH\in\mathcal AH∈A, the Hamiltonian;

  3. a convex set S\mathcal SS of states. A state assigns to an observable its expectation ⟨f⟩σ\langle f\rangle_{\sigma}⟨f⟩σ​, linearly and positively, with ⟨1⟩σ=1\langle1\rangle_{\sigma}=1⟨1⟩σ​=1 — on the observables it can read, which for Definition 7.1 is not all of A\mathcal AA: the Gaussian state on R2\R^{2}R2 gives no value to eq2+p2e^{q^{2}+p^{2}}eq2+p2. Its extreme points are the pure states.

The dynamics is generated by HHH: observables evolve by f˙={f,H}\dot f=\pb fHf˙​={f,H} and states by the adjoint equation, which must carry S\mathcal SS into itself, and the two give the same expectations, ⟨ft⟩σ=⟨f⟩σt\langle f_t\rangle_\sigma=\langle f\rangle_{\sigma_t}⟨ft​⟩σ​=⟨f⟩σt​​.

Each clause has been proved for A=C∞(M)\mathcal A=C^{\infty}(\phase)A=C∞(M) and the states of Definition 7.1: the algebra is a Poisson algebra (Theorem 5.5 and Proposition 5.3); the flow acts on it by automorphisms (Theorem B, Theorem 6.4); the states are convex with the Dirac states as extreme points (Proposition 7.2); for a complete flow the transported state is again a state (§7.3); and the two pictures agree (Theorem 7.6).
